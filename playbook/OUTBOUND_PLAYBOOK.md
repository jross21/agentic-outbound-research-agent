# Outbound Playbook

This document encodes the judgment that separates outbound that earns a reply from outbound that earns a block. It is the standard the agent reasons against — both when deciding what evidence is worth gathering and when drafting a sequence. The bar is simple: **every touch must be something a busy, senior buyer would find worth their thirty seconds.** If a sentence could have been sent to a hundred other companies unchanged, it fails.

## The one rule: relevance over volume

Outbound does not have a volume problem; it has a relevance problem. A hundred generic emails produce annoyance and unsubscribes. Five genuinely relevant, well-timed messages produce meetings. The whole point of researching an account before writing is to earn the right to a specific, useful first sentence. The agent's job is not to "personalize" by dropping a company name into a template — that is mail-merge wearing a costume. The job is to find a real reason this account, this person, and this moment line up with what we sell, and to say it plainly.

## The "why-you / why-now" test

Before any sequence is written, two questions must have grounded answers:

- **Why you?** What about *this* account makes our product relevant — their model, their stage, their stack, their motion, their pain? "You're a B2B SaaS company" is not a why-you; thousands of companies clear that bar. "You just moved to usage-based billing, which makes product-usage data central to how you forecast" is a why-you, because it ties a specific fact about them to a specific reason we matter.
- **Why now?** What changed recently that makes this a good moment rather than a random one? Funding, a key hire, a product launch, a leadership change, a move upmarket, a new market entry. Timing is the difference between "I should look at this" and "not now." A why-now without a real event is just pressure, and buyers can smell manufactured urgency.

If the agent cannot answer both with cited evidence, the right move is to keep researching — or to flag the account as low-signal rather than fabricate a reason.

## Timing signals worth chasing — and the noise to ignore

Not every "signal" is a buying signal. Chase the ones that change a buyer's priorities or budget:

- **Funding rounds** — new capital usually funds GTM expansion and tooling; a strong why-now, especially Series A→C.
- **Leadership hires in the buyer's function** — a new CRO, VP RevOps, or VP Product arrives with a mandate to change things and a fresh budget. The single best signal for a function-specific pitch.
- **Hiring waves in the relevant team** — open RevOps/sales/data roles say the team is being built out and the pain is acute right now.
- **Product launches and pricing changes** — especially ones that change who the buyer is or how they measure success (e.g., usage-based billing makes usage data strategic).
- **Go-to-market motion shifts** — moving upmarket, adding a sales-led motion to a PLG base, entering a new segment.

Ignore the noise: vanity awards, generic "thought leadership," routine blog posts, conference attendance, and anything older than a couple of quarters. Stale signals read as lazy ("congrats on your Series A" eighteen months later is worse than saying nothing). A signal is only useful if it is recent and if it plausibly changes what the buyer cares about.

## Grounding: no claim without a citation

This is non-negotiable and it is the product's spine. Every factual assertion in outreach must trace to a real source the agent actually retrieved — a page it fetched, an enrichment record, a CRM note. The agent maintains an evidence ledger for exactly this reason: a claim that is not in the ledger cannot enter a touch. The failure mode we refuse to ship is the confident hallucination — "I saw you just expanded into Europe" when they did no such thing. One fabricated detail destroys trust permanently and is worse than a blander but true message. When in doubt, say less and stay true.

Cited specificity is also what makes a message feel hand-written. "Saw you closed a $120M Series C in April to scale your commercial org" lands because it is precise and verifiable. The buyer can tell the difference between research and a guess.

## What gets a reply vs. what gets deleted

**Replies come from messages that are:**

- **Short.** Three to five sentences. A senior buyer skims; respect that. Length signals that you value your message more than their time.
- **Specific in the first line.** Lead with the relevant, cited observation, not "I hope this finds you well" or "I'm reaching out because." The first sentence either earns the second or loses the reader.
- **About them, then about us.** Open with their situation; connect to our value in one line; do not pitch features. The buyer cares about their problem, not our product tour.
- **One clear, low-friction ask.** "Worth a 20-minute look?" beats "Let me know a good time for a 45-minute demo with our solutions team." Make saying yes cheap.
- **Human in tone.** Plain language, no jargon stacks, no "synergize." Write like a smart colleague, not a press release.

**Deletes come from:**

- Generic openers that could go to anyone.
- Multiple asks, or a hard ask before any value is shown.
- Feature lists and adjectives ("leading," "innovative," "best-in-class").
- Fake familiarity or manufactured urgency.
- Long paragraphs and walls of text.
- Obvious template seams ("Hi {FirstName}, I see {Company} is in the {Industry} space").

## Sequencing: a cadence, not a barrage

A sequence is a small number of varied, escalating touches — not the same message resent. Three to four touches across email and LinkedIn over a week or two is plenty for a first attempt. Each touch should add something: a new angle, a second signal, a relevant proof point. Vary the channel and keep each touch standalone-readable. The follow-ups are not "just bumping this" — they earn their place by adding a reason. If there is nothing new to say, do not send.

The first touch carries the strongest why-now and the core why-you. The middle touch can come from a different angle — their tech stack, their motion — and a different channel. The final touch offers a second timing reason or a concrete, relevant outcome ("similar teams got there in weeks, not a data-engineering project") and a graceful exit.

## Persona discipline

Write to the person, not the logo. A CRO cares about forecast accuracy, predictable pipeline, and retention. A Head of RevOps cares about tool sprawl, trustworthy data, and fast time-to-value. A VP Product cares about turning usage into activation and closing the loop with GTM. The same account warrants different framing for different personas — lead with the priority that person owns, and pick the value proposition that maps to it. Selecting the right contact matters as much as the message: aim for the person who owns the problem and the budget, and be honest in the rationale when the best available contact is only an approximation.

## The standard, restated

Would this make a smart, busy buyer stop and think "this person actually did their homework, and they might be able to help"? If yes, send it. If it reads like it was generated for ten thousand companies, it was — start over.
